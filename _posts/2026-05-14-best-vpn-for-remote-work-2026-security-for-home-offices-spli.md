## Why Remote Workers Still Need a VPN in 2026

A VPN is not a complete cybersecurity solution, but it remains useful for remote work when used correctly. It encrypts internet traffic between your device and the VPN server, helps protect activity on untrusted networks, and can reduce exposure when working from coffee shops, airports, hotels, or shared housing connections.

For home offices, the benefit is a little different. Your home Wi-Fi may be password-protected, but that does not automatically mean every device on the network is secure. Smart TVs, IoT devices, old routers, and shared family computers can all increase risk. A reputable VPN can add a layer of privacy and help isolate work traffic from some local network risks, though it should be combined with endpoint protection, strong passwords, multi-factor authentication, and regular software updates.

Remote workers generally use VPNs for three reasons:

1. **Privacy on public or shared networks** — especially while traveling.
2. **Secure access to company resources** — such as internal apps, file servers, or admin dashboards.
3. **Location flexibility** — for accessing services that may behave differently by region, though workplace rules and local laws still apply.

For many individuals and small teams, a consumer VPN like Surfshark, NordVPN, ExpressVPN, Proton VPN, or Mullvad may be enough for safer browsing and basic privacy. For companies managing employee access to internal systems, a business VPN, secure access service edge platform, or Zero Trust Network Access tool may be more appropriate.

## What to Look for in a Remote Work VPN

The best VPN for remote work depends on your risk level, job type, and whether you are buying for yourself or a team. A freelance designer working from home has different needs than a system administrator accessing production infrastructure.

Here are the most important features to evaluate.

### Strong Encryption and Modern Protocols

Look for VPNs that support modern, well-regarded protocols such as WireGuard and OpenVPN. WireGuard is usually faster and more efficient, while OpenVPN remains widely trusted and configurable. Some providers also offer proprietary protocols, such as ExpressVPN’s Lightway or NordVPN’s NordLynx. These can be excellent, but they require trust in the provider’s implementation and transparency.

Avoid outdated protocols like PPTP. They are not suitable for secure remote work.

### Kill Switch

A kill switch blocks internet traffic if the VPN connection drops. This matters when working with sensitive files, client portals, admin dashboards, or internal tools. Without a kill switch, your traffic may briefly fall back to your regular ISP connection without obvious warning.

For remote workers, the best VPN apps offer both a system-wide kill switch and app-specific controls. Test this feature before relying on it during travel.

### Split Tunneling

Split tunneling lets you choose which apps or websites go through the VPN and which use your normal internet connection. This is one of the most useful features for remote work.

For example, you might route your browser, email app, and file transfer tools through the VPN while allowing video conferencing software to use your normal connection for lower latency. Or you may need a company app to use a corporate VPN while personal streaming or smart home traffic stays outside it.

Split tunneling can improve performance, but it also creates security trade-offs. If you exclude the wrong app or browser, sensitive traffic may bypass the VPN. For regulated work, your employer may prohibit split tunneling entirely because it can weaken network controls.

### DNS Leak Protection

A VPN should prevent DNS leaks, which occur when your device sends website lookup requests outside the encrypted VPN tunnel. DNS leaks can reveal browsing destinations to your internet provider or local network operator even if the rest of your traffic is encrypted.

Most reputable VPNs include DNS leak protection, but it is worth testing with reputable leak test tools after setup.

### Multi-Device Support

Remote workers often use multiple devices: work laptop, personal laptop, mobile phone, tablet, and sometimes a backup machine. Surfshark’s unlimited device policy is a major convenience here. Other providers may limit connections to a set number of devices, though the limits are often enough for a single person.

For small teams, do not simply share one consumer VPN login across employees unless the vendor’s terms allow it and your security requirements are minimal. Business plans usually provide better admin controls, onboarding, offboarding, billing, and auditing.

### Transparent Privacy Policy and Audits

A VPN provider can technically see more than you might expect, depending on how its systems are designed. That is why a clear no-logs policy, independent audits, and transparent legal disclosures matter.

However, “no logs” does not mean “no data at all.” VPN providers may process account details, payment records, device diagnostics, aggregate usage metrics, crash reports, or anti-abuse data. Read the privacy policy carefully and verify current audit claims at the vendor site.

## Split Tunneling for Remote Work: Helpful, but Use Carefully

Split tunneling is one of the most misunderstood VPN features. It can be a productivity boost, but it can also weaken your protection if configured casually.

### Good Uses for Split Tunneling

Split tunneling is helpful when you want to reduce VPN load or avoid routing unnecessary traffic through an encrypted tunnel. Common examples include:

- Letting Zoom, Microsoft Teams, or Google Meet bypass the VPN for smoother calls.
- Routing only your browser or password manager traffic through the VPN while leaving local apps alone.
- Keeping local printer or NAS access available while connected to a VPN.
- Using a work VPN for internal systems while personal apps use your regular connection.
- Avoiding issues with banking apps or services that block VPN traffic.

This can be especially useful if your VPN server is far away or if your connection is not fast enough to handle everything through the tunnel.

### Risky Uses for Split Tunneling

Split tunneling becomes risky when sensitive work apps are excluded by accident. For example, if your browser bypasses the VPN, then webmail, cloud storage, admin panels, and client dashboards may also bypass the VPN unless you use a separate browser profile or app-based rules.

It can also create problems on untrusted Wi-Fi. If only some traffic is protected, other apps may still communicate over the local network or public hotspot. This does not automatically mean your data is exposed, especially if apps use HTTPS, but it reduces the VPN’s protective value.

For company-managed devices, employees should follow IT policy. Some organizations disable split tunneling to reduce attack paths between local networks and corporate resources. Others allow it for performance reasons but enforce strict device posture, endpoint detection, DNS filtering, and identity controls.

## Business VPN Alternatives: When a Consumer VPN Is Not Enough

A consumer VPN is useful for privacy and safer browsing, but it does not replace a business security architecture. If you manage a team, handle regulated data, or need to control access to internal apps, consider alternatives designed for business environments.

### Business VPNs

A business VPN gives employees encrypted access to company resources. Unlike consumer VPNs, business VPNs usually include admin dashboards, user management, access policies, static IP options, and sometimes dedicated gateways.

They are useful if your company still hosts internal resources on private networks or cloud environments. However, traditional VPNs can grant broad network access once a user connects, which may be more access than the user actually needs.

### Zero Trust Network Access

Zero Trust Network Access, or ZTNA, is increasingly preferred for remote teams. Instead of connecting users to an entire network, ZTNA grants access to specific applications based on identity, device health, location, risk signals, and policy.

This can reduce lateral movement if an account or device is compromised. It also works well for cloud-first companies that do not have a traditional office network.

ZTNA tools may be more complex to deploy, but they are often a better long-term fit for companies with contractors, distributed teams, and sensitive systems.

### SASE and Secure Web Gateways

Secure Access Service Edge platforms combine networking and security functions, often including secure web gateways, cloud firewalls, DNS filtering, data loss prevention, and ZTNA. These are usually aimed at businesses rather than individual remote workers.

If your organization needs centralized control over browsing, SaaS access, malware filtering, and data movement, a SASE-style platform may be more appropriate than asking employees to install consumer VPN apps.

### Remote Desktop and Cloud Workspace Security

Some remote workers access a virtual desktop or cloud workspace rather than downloading files locally. In these setups, VPNs may still be useful, but identity security, device compliance, and session monitoring become more important.

For example, a contractor using a managed virtual desktop may not need a consumer VPN for the actual work session if the platform already encrypts traffic and enforces strong authentication. But they may still benefit from a VPN when using public Wi-Fi for general browsing.

## Home Office Security Checklist

A VPN works best as one layer in a broader security setup. For remote work in 2026, consider the following checklist:

- Use a password manager and unique passwords for every work account.
- Enable multi-factor authentication, preferably with an authenticator app, passkey, or hardware security key.
- Keep your operating system, browser, router firmware, and apps updated.
- Use endpoint protection or antivirus software where appropriate.
- Secure your home Wi-Fi with WPA2 or WPA3 and a strong router password.
- Replace outdated routers that no longer receive security updates.
- Separate IoT devices from work devices using a guest network if your router supports it.
- Turn on your VPN kill switch before working on public Wi-Fi.
- Test for DNS and IP leaks after configuring your VPN.
- Follow employer policies on VPN use, split tunneling, and data handling.
- Avoid saving work files on personal devices unless permitted.
- Lock your screen when away from your desk, even at home.

No VPN can guarantee security, especially if a device is infected with malware or an attacker steals your credentials. The biggest remote work risks often come from phishing, weak passwords, unpatched software, and misconfigured cloud permissions.

## Cons to Consider

Even the best VPN for remote work has limitations. Before subscribing, keep these drawbacks in mind.

### 1. VPNs Can Slow Down Your Connection

A VPN adds encryption and routes traffic through an additional server. With a high-quality provider and nearby server, the slowdown may be small, but it can still affect video calls, large uploads, remote desktop sessions, and cloud-based creative work.

WireGuard usually helps performance, but results depend on your ISP, router, device, server location, and network congestion.

### 2. Split Tunneling Can Create Security Gaps

Split tunneling is convenient, but it can accidentally send sensitive traffic outside the VPN. If you are not sure which apps handle confidential data, it may be safer to route all traffic through the VPN or follow your company’s IT configuration.

For regulated industries or high-risk roles, split tunneling may be inappropriate unless carefully managed.

### 3. Consumer VPNs Lack Enterprise Controls

Surfshark and similar VPNs are excellent for individual privacy and general remote work protection, but they do not provide the same level of user management, device posture checks, audit logs, role-based access, or app-specific business controls as enterprise tools.

If you need to onboard and offboard employees, enforce access policies, or monitor compliance, look at business VPNs, ZTNA, or SASE platforms.

### 4. Some Websites and Apps Block VPN Traffic

Banks, streaming platforms, workplace portals, and fraud-sensitive services may challenge or block VPN connections. You may see extra CAPTCHAs, login verification prompts, or temporary access issues.

Split tunneling can help, but it may reduce protection for excluded apps.

### 5. You Still Have to Trust the VPN Provider

A VPN shifts some trust from your ISP or local network to the VPN provider. That makes the provider’s jurisdiction, ownership, privacy policy, infrastructure, and audit history important.

No VPN review should treat “no logs” as magic. Always read the current privacy policy and verify recent audit claims at the vendor site.

## Setup Tips for Remote Workers

Once you choose a VPN, take a few minutes to configure it properly.

First, install the official app from the vendor’s website or your device’s trusted app store. Avoid third-party download sites. Then enable the kill switch and choose WireGuard or the provider’s recommended modern protocol unless you have a reason to use OpenVPN.

Next, test your connection. Search for a reputable DNS leak and IP leak test tool and confirm that your visible IP address and DNS servers change when the VPN is connected. If you use split tunneling, test each app you plan to include or exclude.

For work devices, ask your employer before installing a consumer VPN. Some companies prohibit non-approved VPN software because it can interfere with monitoring, compliance, or corporate VPN tools. If your company already provides a VPN, use that for work systems unless IT says otherwise.

If you use a VPN router, remember that router-level VPNs can protect devices that do not support VPN apps, but they are harder to configure and may reduce performance. They also make split tunneling more complicated unless your router firmware supports policy-based routing.
