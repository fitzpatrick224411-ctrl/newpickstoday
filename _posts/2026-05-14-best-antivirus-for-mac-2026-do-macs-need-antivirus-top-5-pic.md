## Do Macs Actually Need Antivirus in 2026?

Short answer: yes, more than ever — though the reasoning is nuanced.

For years, Macs occupied a small enough market share that cybercriminals didn't bother targeting them heavily. That calculus has changed. Apple Silicon Macs have driven a surge in Mac adoption, and with more users comes more attacker interest. Malware targeting macOS — including adware, spyware, ransomware, and info-stealers — has grown meaningfully over the past few years. Threats like the AMOS (Atomic macOS Stealer) infostealer, which targets crypto wallets and stored passwords, demonstrated clearly in 2023 and into subsequent years that macOS is absolutely on attackers' radar. (Verify current threat statistics at AV-TEST or AV-Comparatives for the latest data.)

Apple has built some impressive native defenses into macOS:

- **XProtect** — Apple's built-in signature-based malware scanner, updated silently in the background.
- **Gatekeeper** — Prevents unauthorized or unsigned apps from running without user permission.
- **System Integrity Protection (SIP)** — Locks down critical system files from modification, even by privileged processes.
- **Notarization** — Requires developers to submit apps to Apple for security checks before distribution.

These protections are genuinely solid for baseline defense. But they have real limits. XProtect's signatures lag behind cutting-edge threats. Gatekeeper can be bypassed (users often override it themselves). And none of Apple's native tools offer real-time web protection, phishing defense for browsers, VPN integration, identity monitoring, or the kind of behavioral detection that catches zero-day threats before Apple updates its definitions.

If you do any online banking, use your Mac for work, store sensitive files, or simply want peace of mind, a third-party antivirus adds meaningful layers of protection that Apple doesn't cover.

## What to Look for When Choosing Mac Antivirus

Not all antivirus software is created equal, and a few criteria should guide your decision beyond just marketing claims:

**Independent Lab Scores** — Check AV-TEST (av-test.org) and AV-Comparatives (av-comparatives.org) for current macOS test results. These organizations conduct rigorous, methodology-transparent evaluations. Don't trust detection rate claims that come only from the vendor itself.

**System Performance Impact** — macOS users are sensitive to anything that slows down their machine, and rightly so. Look for products that score well on performance benchmarks in independent tests, not just protection scores.

**Privacy Policy** — This is especially critical for security software, which by definition runs with elevated privileges on your machine. Read what data the product collects, whether it shares that data, and what your opt-out rights are. The Avast/Jumpshot incident is a reminder this isn't hypothetical.

**macOS Version Compatibility** — Make sure the software supports your current macOS version (including Apple Silicon M-series chips natively, not just via Rosetta translation). Check compatibility before purchasing.

**Renewal Pricing** — Many antivirus vendors offer steep introductory discounts and then renew at significantly higher rates. Check the renewal price, not just the first-year price, before committing.

## Limitations

No antivirus product — however good — is a silver bullet. Here are real limitations to keep in mind:

1. **Zero-day gaps exist across all products.** Signature-based detection (which most products still rely on heavily) has a fundamental delay: the threat must be identified and cataloged before your scanner can catch it. Behavioral detection helps, but it's not perfect either.

2. **Mac antivirus can create false positives.** Aggressive scanning engines sometimes flag legitimate software, particularly niche utilities or apps from smaller developers. This can interrupt workflows and create unnecessary alarm. Malwarebytes and Intego tend to be better calibrated for Mac software than Windows-first solutions.

3. **Performance impact is real, even if minor.** Even the lightest Mac antivirus products add some overhead — background scanning, real-time file monitoring, and network filtering all consume CPU and RAM. On older or entry-level Macs, this can be noticeable.

4. **No antivirus replaces good habits.** Phishing attacks, social engineering, and tricking users into voluntarily running malicious software bypass most technical defenses entirely. An antivirus is one layer of protection, not a substitute for skepticism about unsolicited emails, sketchy downloads, or "urgent" browser pop-ups.

5. **Privacy trade-offs are real.** Every antivirus product scans your files and, to varying degrees, your network traffic. You are granting that software significant access to your data. Choose a vendor with a transparent, favorable privacy policy — and audit it periodically.
