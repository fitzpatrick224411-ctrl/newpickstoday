## Who Is Proton and Why Does It Matter?

Proton AG is a Swiss company that originally made its name with ProtonMail, an end-to-end encrypted email service launched in 2014 and built by scientists who met at CERN. That origin story matters more than it might seem. The company was founded with a genuine privacy-first philosophy — not as a selling point retrofitted after the fact, but as the entire reason for its existence.

Proton VPN launched in 2017, and unlike many VPN providers that are owned by larger conglomerates or have murky ownership structures, Proton operates as an independent Swiss company. Switzerland's data protection laws are among the strongest in the world, and the country sits outside the jurisdiction of both the EU's legal frameworks and the US-dominated intelligence alliances (the 5/9/14 Eyes). Proton has repeatedly emphasized that Swiss law does not compel them to log or hand over user data, and they've pointed to specific court cases where they successfully resisted overreaching data requests.

This isn't just marketing fluff. Proton VPN is fully open source — all client applications have been made publicly available on GitHub, and the code has been independently audited by third-party security firms (verify current audit details at the vendor site). That's a meaningful level of transparency that many competitors, including some very well-known names, still don't offer.

## Privacy Credentials: How Proton VPN Holds Up Under Scrutiny

This is where Proton genuinely differentiates itself from the VPN market crowd. Let's go through the major pillars:

**No-logs policy — and it's been tested.** Proton VPN has stated clearly that it does not log user connection data, IP addresses, or browsing activity. More importantly, this has been stress-tested in real legal situations. When Swiss authorities requested data in the past, Proton was able to demonstrate it had nothing meaningful to hand over. That's the ideal outcome of a true no-logs architecture.

**Open-source code and third-party audits.** Every single client app — Windows, macOS, Linux, iOS, Android — has been published openly. Security researchers can and do review the code. Regular independent audits have been completed (verify the most recent audit dates and firms on the Proton website). This is the gold standard for transparency.

**Secure Core architecture.** On the paid plan, Secure Core routes your traffic through privacy-friendly countries (Iceland, Switzerland, or Sweden) before hitting the regular VPN server. Even if a final VPN server were somehow compromised, your original IP address would remain protected. This is a genuine defense-in-depth feature, not just a name.

**Swiss jurisdiction.** This isn't theoretical. Switzerland has a history of upholding its data protection laws against foreign government requests, and Proton has leaned into this hard — operating under the Swiss Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP) and resisting attempts to force data disclosure.

**Past controversy to know about:** In 2021, Proton Mail received significant criticism when it complied with a Swiss court order by providing the IP address of a French climate activist to authorities. This was a Proton *Mail* case, not a Proton VPN case, and Proton argued it had no legal basis to refuse a valid Swiss court order in that specific instance. The incident does serve as a useful reminder: no privacy tool is a magic shield, and Proton's "no-logs" defense only works within the bounds of what they technically collect. For VPN specifically, the no-logs policy means there's much less to hand over in the first place.

Streaming performance on paid plans is solid. Proton VPN can unblock major platforms including Netflix US, BBC iPlayer, and others (verify current streaming support on the vendor site, as these capabilities change frequently). The free tier should not be expected to reliably unblock streaming — the server range is too limited.

Torrenting is reserved entirely for paid users. P2P traffic is blocked on free servers, which is a common limitation but worth flagging clearly.

The app design has matured significantly over the past few years. The interface is clean, settings are accessible without being overwhelming, and the kill switch functions reliably across platforms. Auto-connect, split tunneling, and always-on VPN are all present on paid plans.

## Cons to Consider

No VPN is perfect, and Proton VPN has some genuine limitations worth knowing before you commit:

**1. Free plan server congestion.** With only a handful of free server locations shared among a large free user base, speeds during busy periods can drop noticeably. If you're in a timezone where US and European servers are heavily used, you may find the experience frustrating for anything beyond basic browsing.

**2. Paid plan pricing isn't the cheapest.** Proton VPN Plus is priced at a premium compared to budget-focused competitors. If cost is your primary consideration, you can find technically comparable VPNs for less — though the privacy pedigree and open-source transparency are harder to replicate at that lower price point. Always verify current pricing at the vendor site, as promotional rates change.

**3. No-logs only goes so far.** As discussed in the ProtonMail 2021 incident, Swiss law can compel compliance under specific circumstances. For VPN specifically the risk is much lower because less data is collected — but it's intellectually dishonest to claim any VPN provides absolute legal immunity. No VPN does.

**4. Streaming on free is unreliable.** If your main goal is accessing geo-restricted streaming content, the free plan is not the right tool. The limited server options make consistent unblocking essentially impossible on the free tier.

**5. Simultaneous connections on free is just one device.** If you want to cover your phone and laptop simultaneously on the free plan, you can't. You'd need to manually switch the VPN connection, which is inconvenient in practice.
